1. Introduction & Objectives

The primary goal of this setup was to establish stable deposition recipes for four key processes: SiO2, SiN, a-Si, and CH4 plasma for surface modification. In CVD processes, Uniformity is the most critical metric for ensuring reliable device performance across a wafer.

Target Specifications
  • SiO2: Within-wafer uniformity < 3%
  • SiN: Within-wafer uniformity < 4%
  • a-Si: Establishing stable deposition gas conditions.
  • CH4 Plasma: Achieving rapid plasma ignition and stable matching for surface treatment.

During the initial setup, plasma stability was monitored by analyzing the Reflect Power. By optimizing the impedance matching between the ICP and RF generators, I aimed to minimize reflect power as quickly as possible to ensure process repeatability.

2. Experimental Approach: Data-Driven Optimization

2.1 SiO2 Process Stabilization (44 Test Runs)

The optimization of SiO2 was the most rigorous, involving 44 separate test runs. The process utilized SiH4 and O2 as primary precursors, with Ar added to tune the plasma density and uniformity.

Key Parameters Investigated
  • Pressure & APC (Automatic Pressure Control)
  • ICP & RF (Substrate) Power
  • Chuck Temperature & Deposition Time

Thickness was measured at 9 points across an 8-inch wafer to calculate Within-wafer Uniformity. To ensure mass-production readiness, I also conducted Run-to-Run (R2R) tests to verify that uniformity remained consistent over multiple batches.

2.2 Correlation Matrix and Plasma Insights

Rather than relying solely on trial and error, I utilized Python (sklearn) to generate a Correlation Matrix. This allowed me to visualize how variables like Pressure, O2 flow, and RF power interacted to affect uniformity.

Major Findings from the Analysis
  • Gas Ratio Impact: Absolute flow rates were less critical than the SiH4/O2 ratio. High SiH4 concentrations led to center-thick deposition, while high O2 concentrations shifted the thickness to the edge due to the ICP skin effect.
  • Pressure & Energy: High pressure increased plasma density but lowered ion energy. I identified an optimization point based on the Paschen Curve to balance ion bombardment and density.
  • RF Power (Substrate): Low RF power allowed more radicals to exist at the surface, while high power increased sputtering effects, which could either improve or degrade uniformity depending on the energy balance.

3. Results: Achieving the 2.7% Uniformity Baseline

By integrating theoretical plasma behavior with statistical analysis, I moved away from the standard "out-of-the-box" recipes provided by the manufacturer.

The Optimized Recipe
  • High Pressure / Low RF Power
  • Low SiH4 Flow / High O2 Flow

Result: Successfully achieved 2.7% Uniformity for SiO2, surpassing the initial 3% target.

4. Reflections: Engineering Insights & Lessons Learned

Multivariate Complexity & Holistic Design

ICP-CVD setup is a masterclass in managing intercorrelated variables. I learned that a single adjustment—such as the SiH4/O2 ratio—can completely invert a deposition profile from convex to concave. This project reinforced that achieving high-precision uniformity requires a systemic approach rather than isolated parameter tuning.

Hardware-Specific Architecture

A key takeaway was the impact of subtle hardware differences. Even with identical process conditions, the unique chuck height in our TEOS-capable system significantly altered the plasma environment compared to standard models. It taught me that an engineer must deeply understand the specific physical architecture of the chamber to translate "proven" recipes into actual results.

Synergy of Data Science and Plasma Physics

Integrating a Correlation Matrix (sklearn) with theoretical plasma physics was a game-changer. While the data identified "what" was happening, my understanding of the skin effect and Paschen curve explained "why." This synergy between statistical analysis and physical insight allowed me to surpass the 3% uniformity target, reaching a robust 2.7% baseline.
